Marijuana

Jay and I are sitting in a bar somewhere in The Hague, drinking beer. Ever since we arrived in Amsterdam yesterday, Jay has been pestering me about wanting to try marijuana, just one joint, just once. It is not as though I am not curious myself. To be honest, I simply do not have the nerve. Not that I am a coward or anything. What scares me is not some textbook issue like marijuana being illegal in Korea, or Korean law applying to Koreans even overseas. Whatever anyone says, marijuana is a drug. That is the problem. I am the kind of person who gets hooked easily on things that are bad for him. I know perfectly well that once I fall into something, I have a hard time crawling back out. There are already too many things I started with a casual “What the hell, I’ll try it once,” only to find I could not quit. My life is already that of a washout pushed right to the edge of a cliff. I am in no position to add “drug addict” to the list.

Jay had recently come out of a long, exhausting divorce battle. At first she had planned on a divorce by mutual consent, but the division of property turned the whole thing into litigation, and only at the very end did she somehow manage to reach a settlement. Jay’s hope that she and her husband might remain good friends after the divorce was smashed to pieces. She should never have entertained such a hope in the first place. If it had simply been another woman, perhaps it might not have gone as far as divorce. If he had not set up house with some young bitch, she might have been broad-minded enough to let it slide, saying, “Men do that sort of thing.” In fact, Jay’s husband was not exactly innocent when it came to women. Whenever he came back from a round of golf with his high school friends, Jay could smell something strange on him. At first she thought it was just the cheap lotion and aftershave from the golf club locker room. But that was not it. One day, because the housekeeper’s father had died and the laundry had piled up for several days, Jay was sorting the clothes to run the washing machine when she happened to find a long yellow hair stuck to her husband’s underwear. That night, after a terrific fight with him, Jay called me. And this is what she said.

“That crazy bastard filmed everything he did with me. And he has footage of himself doing it with other bitches too.”

Every night, whenever her husband went into his study, he locked the door behind him, and that had bothered her. So one day she searched his computer from top to bottom. “You said you don’t know how to copy files. Stop wasting time and come now.”

With a dishwasher and a robot vacuum cleaner, it was not hard in the slightest. I wanted to call my wife and tell her that from now on I would take care of all the housework. All through our marriage, she had snapped at me countless times, saying she could not stand that kind of attitude of mine. But from my point of view, what could I have said to her? That because I could not divorce her, I was running away to Paris with a female friend? She would have called me insane, obviously. But if anyone knew my circumstances, no one could blame me. First, my wife had agreed to three months of separation and then tried to shove divorce papers at me after only two months. That was a clear breach of contract. I had been to Paris once before. Back in college, like everyone else, I had spent about two weeks going around Europe—Paris, London, Rome, places like that. Of course I had not traveled alone. Naturally, I had gone on a package tour. Then as now, going abroad alone did not appeal to me. What was I supposed to do in a place where I did not speak the language if I sprained my ankle or got a stomachache? Go to a hospital? Fine. Let us say a hospital could solve that. But what if I had all my money stolen by a pickpocket on the subway in Rome, or ran into a mugger in some back alley in Paris? I really do not even want to imagine it. People may curse me, saying what kind of man has so little guts. That is all right. That is simply the kind of man I am. It is not pickpockets or muggers I am afraid of. It is the annoying mess that would follow. I cannot stand troublesome things.

It was not as if Jay and I had come to Paris with some grand travel plan. Like everyone else, we went up the Eiffel Tower, looked at the Arc de Triomphe, and walked along the Champs-Élysées. We stopped at a few cathedrals listed in the travel book. No matter how many cathedrals you look at, a cathedral is still just a cathedral. When we got hungry on our walks, we went into nice bakeries and ate our fill of fragrant French bread. Once I bought a pastry thinking it was filled with sweet red bean paste, only to discover it was packed with chocolate, which put me in a bad mood for a while, but for the most part the days were pleasant. In Paris, life became active. We walked and walked every day. I had been sitting still for too long. Perhaps it was because we had managed well enough on my wife’s income alone. If I had held down a regular job like other people, perhaps things would not have come to this. I have the perfectly ordinary constitution of a man whose body instinctively rejects the word “workplace.” Unfortunately, I have neither business sense nor business skill, so there is nothing else for me to do. When my wife said that doing nothing would be the best help I could give, perhaps she was not entirely wrong. At first business was good. But who could have known that within a few months every first floor of every building would have a café in it? When big corporate franchises too numerous to memorize came charging in as if their lives depended on it, what was I supposed to do? Retirees were pouring in their severance pay, even their entire savings. How could a decent person like me, someone who had hoped to run a refined little business listening to music and drinking coffee at leisure, compete with that? If I had known things would go that way, I would never have started. It was lucky enough that I did not take a huge loss. As soon as I closed the café, my wife brought up divorce. Our relationship—neither marriage nor separation nor divorce—was already rushing toward disaster. If my wife had another man, I might have felt more at ease, but that was not the case either. If that man could have made her happy, I might easily have agreed to divorce. Love, too, once the foam has gone flat, is not much different from stale beer. When you meet dressed nicely, with makeup on and good clothes, everything is lovely; but once you get married and step into daily life, even good feelings are bound to go dull. My wife must know. She must know there is no one on this earth who loves her more than I do. Still, it is not as if I had no complaints about her at all. I cannot stand the way my wife raises her little finger when she holds a pen. Even when she picks up a teacup, she always sticks out that little finger. In the old days, I found it cute and lovable.
