1. Is There More Light in a Room Lined with Mirrors?

I imagined lighting identical candles in two rooms: an ordinary room and one surrounded on all sides by mirrors. If the two candles were equally bright, they would emit the same amount of light energy over a given period. Yet a comparison of the total amount of light present in the two rooms at a particular moment could reveal a difference.

Ordinary walls reflect some light and absorb a substantial portion of it. The absorbed light energy is converted into the internal energy of the materials that make up the walls. Highly reflective mirrors return light repeatedly, increasing the average time photons remain inside the room.

Consequently, even when the same amount of light is emitted, more light energy may be present in the mirrored room at a given moment. This led me to distinguish between two quantities. One is the light energy produced by a candle over a certain period; the other is the light energy present inside the room at a particular instant. The latter depends on the rate at which light is produced and the length of time it remains in the room.

Once I understood this distinction, I became interested in a space capable of preserving light for long periods. By reducing the loss of light, it might be possible to accumulate a large amount of light within a space using the same energy source. I then wondered what would happen if light could be confined perfectly.

2. What Happens If Light Is Confined Perfectly?

Next, I imagined an ideal room whose walls absorbed no light at all and which had no opening through which light could escape. If a candle continued to emit light inside it, the newly introduced energy would accumulate. Under conditions of zero loss, the longer the candle remained lit, the greater the amount of accumulated light energy would become.

In reality, creating a perfectly reflecting wall is difficult. Actual mirrors absorb or transmit light at certain wavelengths. The air and the candle inside the room also interact with light. In real optical cavities, such losses are described using measures such as photon lifetime and the quality factor.

For the thought experiment, I assumed an extreme situation in which all these losses were eliminated. This allowed me to shift my attention from the problem of producing energy to that of preserving it. Initially, I imagined a perfectly mirrored room made of physical materials. Later, I considered whether space itself could serve as a boundary that confines energy. This idea became the starting point for the energy storage space that appears in the novel.

3. Can a Single Photon Exist Forever?

While considering ways to confine light, I also became curious about the fate of an individual photon. If a photon were reflected indefinitely inside an enclosed space, could we say that the same photon continued to exist? Here, it was necessary to distinguish between a situation in which light cannot pass through a wall and one in which the wall does not absorb it. Even if light cannot penetrate a wall, it may still be absorbed by the atoms or electrons composing it. Its energy can then heat the material or produce other internal states. The wall may subsequently emit light again, but that process is described as the emission of a new photon.

A photon is a quantized excitation of the electromagnetic field. Given this property, there are limits to imagining an individual photon as a small object that retains a fixed shape. In an ideal perfectly reflecting cavity, one can assume a situation in which a single-photon state is maintained indefinitely, provided that there are no interactions with the outside and the walls remain stationary. Such a thought experiment helped me distinguish between the conservation of energy and the physical state of a photon.

From this point on, my interest in the storage space also changed. Rather than preserving individual photons in their original form for long periods, the more important question became how to maintain the energy carried by light and the physical states it forms.

4. Does Time Not Pass for a Photon?

Thinking about the lifetime of a photon led me to examine the concept of time in relativity. It is often said that time does not pass for a photon. I needed to establish what this expression actually means.

An object with mass can have a reference frame moving along with it. A person aboard a spacecraft, for example, can measure elapsed time using a wristwatch. In relativity, this is called proper time. A photon travels at the speed of light in a vacuum, so a valid inertial rest frame for a photon cannot be defined. Along the lightlike path followed by a photon, the proper-time interval is zero, but this cannot be interpreted as meaning that the photon experiences its departure and arrival simultaneously.

This examination helped me distinguish between the travel time of light and the time measured by an observer watching it. It also raised the question of whether time flowing outside the storage space and time within that space would need to be considered separately. This question about time remained independent for some time. It became important again when I later considered a structure in which an action in the future could affect the galaxy of the past.

5. Can a Civilization Run Out of Energy Even Though Energy Is Conserved?

The idea of storing light for long periods led to questions about the nature of energy itself. Energy can be transformed into various forms. The chemical energy of fuel is converted into motion and heat, while nuclear fusion in stars converts some mass into radiant energy and other forms of energy. When light is absorbed by matter, its energy changes the internal state of that matter.

The important distinction here was between the total amount of energy and the energy that can actually be used. Heat flows between objects at different temperatures. This difference can be used to perform work. As the two objects approach thermal equilibrium, their capacity to perform work in the same way diminishes. Even when energy exists, making use of it requires suitable temperature differences, density differences, and arrangements of matter.

To understand this problem, I examined the concepts of entropy and available energy. At first, I thought the important question was how much energy could be secured, but as the investigation progressed, the form in which that energy could be preserved became more important.

This line of thought became connected to the decline of galactic civilization in the novel. I imagined that if a galactic civilization consumed enormous amounts of energy over a long period, its concentrated energy sources and the conditions required to exploit them might gradually diminish, rather than the total amount of energy itself.

6. What Does a Civilization That Uses an Entire Galaxy Consume?

I now expanded the scale of the thought experiment from a single room to an entire galaxy. If humanity had settled in countless star systems and built a civilization using a network of wormholes, its methods of energy consumption would differ greatly from those of the present day. Such a civilization might construct large-scale energy collection facilities around stars, extract matter from stars, draw rotational energy from black holes, and operate immense computing facilities.

What problems might arise if such a civilization continued to grow over a long period? Initially, I imagined the entire galaxy becoming hotter because of human energy consumption. But a galaxy is not an enclosed space surrounded by an atmosphere like Earth. The waste heat emitted by civilization can spread beyond the galaxy in the form of electromagnetic radiation.

I therefore changed the premise. The civilization would consume stellar fuel faster than it would be depleted through natural stellar evolution, collect interstellar gas that might otherwise form new stars, and exploit even the energy stored in black holes. Through these processes, the civilization would obtain the energy needed to sustain its way of life. At the same time, it would alter the long-term distribution of usable energy sources and release previously concentrated energy into the surrounding space in forms that would be difficult to recover.

In the novel, galactic civilization initially attributes these changes to conventional environmental problems and resource depletion. It might consider policies limiting stellar energy use, restricting energy consumption, and prohibiting the exploitation of black holes. This premise developed into a story about a civilization attempting to solve problems of its own making through technology and administrative systems. I also considered the possibility that some of the physical changes already underway might prove difficult to reverse, even if the civilization reduced its consumption.

7. Does Stored Energy Become More Valuable Over Time?

While examining the idea of storing energy for long periods, I once imagined a kind of cosmic compound-interest savings account. What benefit might there be in accumulating energy little by little for the distant future? In economics, compound interest refers to a process in which accumulated assets generate further returns, accelerating the growth of those assets. I wondered whether a similar principle could apply to energy storage.

Physically, energy does not increase merely because it has been stored. The idea of applying compound interest to energy itself therefore had to be revised. Instead, I considered the possibility that energy-collecting devices could be used to expand the capacity for further collection. If one collector used the energy it had acquired to build another collector, and those collectors subsequently built more, the collection network could expand rapidly. The relative value of stored energy could also change over time. Energy accumulated in an era when stars and gas were abundant would become a far more precious resource in the distant future, when most stars had disappeared.

This investigation also altered the function of the storage space in the novel. I began considering both the problem of increasing the amount stored and the problem of keeping that energy usable over long periods.

8. What Problems Arise When Light Is Concentrated in a Small Space?

At this point, I was reminded of a scene from the film Men in Black, which I had seen long ago. It showed a galaxy contained inside a small sphere attached to a cat's collar. When I first saw the scene, I was intrigued by the image itself: an immense world existing inside a tiny object.

After the mirrored-room thought experiment, I began examining that image as a physical problem. Although light differs from objects with rest mass, it possesses energy and momentum. Adding light to a sealed container increases the total mass-energy of the container and its contents. Light also transfers momentum to the walls, increasing the radiation pressure inside.

As the scale of the energy to be stored increases, the problems of gravity and pressure become more serious. Concentrating too much energy within a small volume also requires consideration of the conditions associated with black-hole formation. This revealed a problem with the idea of compressing the energy of an entire galaxy directly into a small sphere.

To resolve it, I changed the relationship between the sphere and the storage space. The small object visible from the outside would serve as an entrance connected to an immense storage space, while the energy itself would be kept in a separate, spacious spacetime. In general relativity, special geometrical models connecting a narrow entrance to a large interior region have been studied. Whether such structures could actually be constructed and maintained in a stable form remains unconfirmed. For the novel, I decided to take theoretically conceivable spacetime structures as a basis and add the technological assumptions needed for the story. In this way, the original sphere acquired a new role.

9. What Should a Separate Spacetime for Storing Energy Look Like?

The idea of using the sphere as an entrance led to questions about the internal structure of the storage space. First, the volume of the storage space would have to be sufficiently large. I also needed to consider how to deal with the gravity and pressure generated by the energy stored inside it.

Initially, I imagined a space in which time did not pass. If the energy inside remained unchanged no matter how much time elapsed in the outside universe, it seemed that such a space would make an ideal storage facility. But in a space where time does not exist at all, it is difficult to define processes through which energy enters or the internal state changes. The accumulation of energy requires a sequence of events.

I therefore changed the premise so that the storage space would have its own independent time. I imagined a structure in which time flowed differently from the outside universe, or one in which an independent cosmic history began after the connection to the outside was closed. During this process, I examined the concept of a baby universe: a new spacetime connected to the existing universe through a narrow passage, receiving energy and information until certain conditions were met, after which it would begin expanding independently. The formation of a baby universe and the maintenance of a stable connection to it are not established technologies in current physics. Several theoretical models explore related possibilities, and I extended some of these ideas for the novel.

I also examined the possibility that if time and the gravitational field inside the storage space differed from those outside, the measured energy of photons could change as they traveled. The hypothesis that some of the stored energy might contribute to the expansion of the interior space also emerged at this stage. As a result, the storage space developed from a simple warehouse for collecting light into a region possessing its own independent physical conditions.

10. What Must Be Stored to Start a New Universe?

As I considered using the storage space as the initial state of a new universe, questions arose about what should actually be stored. Would gathering light energy in one place be sufficient to ensure that stars, planets, and complex material structures could form in the new universe?

Even if the total amount of energy were sufficient, there might be too few usable differences if that energy were distributed in a state close to thermal equilibrium. The formation of stars and planets requires various conditions, including differences in matter density, motion, and gravitational instabilities.

I therefore conceived of a storage space that would contain not only energy but also particular compositions and distributions of matter, temperature differences, and initial density fluctuations. At this stage, the purpose of the storage space changed again. What had begun as a plan to preserve energy for future use developed into a plan to establish the initial conditions of a new universe.

From this perspective, the scientist of the future would have no reason to collect only starlight. The scientist might attempt to acquire energy remaining in stars, interstellar gas, elementary particles, and black holes. It was also necessary to consider how this process would affect the existing galaxy. As the scientist recovered the required energy and matter, the conditions necessary for forming stars and other celestial bodies in the existing universe could deteriorate. This was the point at which preparing the initial conditions of a new universe began to connect with the extinction of the existing galaxy.

11. Can Even Black Holes Be Harvested?

While examining the galaxy's energy reserves, I became interested in black holes. Black holes contain enormous amounts of mass-energy. In theory, some rotational energy can be extracted from a rotating black hole. Black holes are understood to lose energy through Hawking radiation over extremely long periods, but the natural disappearance of a massive black hole would take an extraordinarily long time. Black holes could therefore remain important energy resources in the distant future, even after most ordinary stars had been exhausted.

Initially, I imagined black holes collapsing into the storage space. But since black holes are themselves celestial objects formed through gravitational collapse, I needed to revise that description. I subsequently imagined a situation in which a black hole's rotation slowed, its mass and event horizon changed at an abnormal rate, and even the orbits of surrounding celestial bodies were affected. If such changes could be distinguished from ordinary Hawking radiation, scientists in the galaxy would have to search for another physical cause.

The first phenomenon observed in the novel is the photon deficit. If stars and black holes subsequently underwent abnormal changes, observers might begin investigating the possibility that the galaxy's various energy sources were being systematically depleted. This premise expanded the scale of the events while also changing how galactic civilization attempted to trace their cause.

12. When Does the Photon Deficit Begin Across the Galaxy?

I next needed to determine when the photon deficit would appear in different parts of the galaxy. Initially, it seemed possible to have observatories throughout the galaxy discover the deficit on the same day and at the same time. In relativity, however, defining the simultaneity of distant events requires consideration of reference frames and the time taken for signals to travel.

The galaxy's planets move at different velocities and exist in different gravitational environments. Even if they are connected by a wormhole network, the clocks and calendars used in different regions do not automatically agree. I therefore reconsidered how a single date could be assigned to all the observatories.

In cosmology, physical quantities associated with the expansion of the universe can be used to indicate its age. For example, the average temperature of the cosmic microwave background and the degree of cosmic expansion are used to study how the universe has evolved. On this basis, I examined the possibility of using a condition in which the universe reaches a particular physical state rather than specifying a date. In this scenario, devices in different parts of the galaxy would operate according to the same cosmological condition.

When comparing the observatories' records, it would be necessary to correct for differences in local motion, gravitational environments, and signal travel times. I developed the premise so that, through these corrections, the observers would discover that the onset of the deficit was associated with a single cosmological boundary. This idea also played an important role in conceiving the photon deficit and Galactic Standard Time in A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction. I needed to consider how galactic civilization would compare records based on different time systems and establish the sequence of events.

13. Can the Future Affect the Galaxy of the Past?

An even more difficult problem emerged when I considered the cause of the photon deficit. If a scientist in the distant future collected energy to create a new universe, how could that collection be connected to a photon deficit already being observed in the galaxy of the past?

Initially, I devised a scenario in which the scientist traveled backward through time and installed energy-collection devices in the past. Yet even if traversable wormholes were assumed to exist, traveling freely to any desired point in the past would be subject to various constraints. Methods of creating a time difference between the two mouths of a wormhole have been discussed theoretically, but this does not establish that a wormhole can be installed arbitrarily at a time before it existed.

I therefore reworked the premise of creating new passages in the past. This drew my attention to the wormhole network already used by galactic civilization. If a transportation network connecting countless star systems and settlements had existed for a very long time, the future scientist might be able to make use of those passages. This approach reduced the need to explain how the scientist had constructed every collection inlet and sent it into the past. Instead, the scientist became someone who developed a technology capable of changing the connections between existing passages or redirecting them toward a particular destination.

Of course, the assumption that an action in the future could affect the operation of wormholes existing in the past remained a fictional technology within the novel. To address this problem, I also examined other hypotheses concerning time and causality.
