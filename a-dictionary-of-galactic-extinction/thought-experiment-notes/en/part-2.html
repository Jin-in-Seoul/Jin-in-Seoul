14. What If an Existing Wormhole Network Were Reconnected?

The idea of using the galactic wormhole network significantly changed the novel's temporal structure. In the past, humanity had established settlements in different parts of the galaxy and built a wormhole network connecting star systems. These passages became essential infrastructure for the movement of people and goods, communication, administration, and economic activity. After an immense period had passed, the scientist of the future prepared a new storage space. Using the wormhole network that had existed since the past, the scientist created a structure through which energy could move into that space.

I examined two possibilities. In the first, the mouths of existing wormholes would be redirected to connect with the new storage space. In the second, those connections would be incorporated into the boundary conditions of spacetime as a whole, encompassing both past and future. To understand the latter possibility, I examined the concept of the block universe. If spacetime in relativity is regarded as a single, complete structure, events in the past and future can be interpreted as belonging to one history.

This interpretation does not, by itself, give a scientist in the future the power to manipulate the past. For the novel, I introduced the additional assumption of a technology capable of establishing the connection between the existing wormhole network and the storage space within a complete spacetime structure. Under this premise, the photon deficit in the galaxy of the past occurs because the passages were already connected to the future storage space. Observers in the past do not know where those passages lead. The idea that the transportation network which brought prosperity to the galaxy would, after an immense passage of time, be used to collect energy also arose during this process.

15. How Did Noah's Role Change?

As I examined the structure of time and the wormhole network, the role of Noah Cain also changed. In the initial conception, Noah was the person directly responsible for the photon deficit observed in the galaxy of the past. Having reached the distant future, Noah would construct a device for collecting energy, and that device would cause light to disappear from the galaxy of the past.

Examining this premise raised several questions. How could Noah install devices that had not existed in the past? From what point in time could those devices begin operating? How could the era in which the photon deficit first appeared be connected to the era in which Noah lived? The emergence of the existing wormhole network as a possible solution allowed me to revise Noah's role. Instead of directly installing new collection inlets in the galactic past, Noah became someone who connected existing passages to the energy storage space. The central task shifted to preparing that space and completing the connections.

This change also affected the significance of Noah's actions. The galaxy of the past is already experiencing the photon deficit, and records and hypotheses concerning its cause have accumulated. In the distant future, Noah completes the structure associated with that phenomenon. This process led me to reconsider how the actions of one individual could be connected to the history of a civilization extending across an immense period. Noah's role did not become smaller; it changed into one that connected past and future within a single structure. This change also influenced the conception of the novel's ending. It is an example of an idea from the initial conception taking on a different role after its physical implications had been examined.

16. If an Action in the Future Affects the Past, What Happens to Causality?

A scenario in which Noah's actions are connected to phenomena in the galaxy's past gives rise to the paradoxes of time travel. If a scientist in the distant future collects energy from the galaxy of the past and destroys its civilization, the very history that allowed the scientist to be born and carry out the research might disappear. This would create a contradiction between the cause that led to the device's construction and the result produced by that device. I compared several possible ways of dealing with this problem.

The first was a scenario in which a new branch of the universe emerged whenever the past was changed. The future scientist would affect a different branch of the galaxy rather than the history the scientist had experienced. This could avoid the contradiction of time travel, but it would change the originally conceived relationship between the past and future of the same galaxy.

The second was a scenario in which the past and future were repeatedly altered. Under such a premise, the outcome of events would change each time history was rewritten. To trace the novel's chain of cause and effect to its conclusion, additional rules governing these changes would have to be devised.

The third was a scenario that preserved self-consistency. It would assume that the future scientist's construction of the device and the appearance of the photon deficit in the past had always belonged to the same history. In that case, despite the deficit occurring in the past, the history necessary for the scientist's birth would have to remain intact.

Of these possibilities, I judged the self-consistent model to be the most suitable for the novel's temporal structure. General relativity has provided a framework for theoretical investigations of unusual spacetime structures and the possibility of time travel. Concepts such as the Novikov self-consistency principle and Hawking's chronology protection conjecture were among those I examined to understand the problem.

For the novel, I settled on a hypothesis that allowed a self-consistent history. This made it possible to connect Noah's actions to events in the past without adopting a complicated temporal structure in which history was repeatedly rewritten.

17. How Can Traversable Wormholes Be Kept Open?

After adopting the premise of using an existing wormhole network, I also needed to examine the physical conditions of the wormholes themselves. General relativity permits special geometrical solutions connecting different regions of spacetime. Research has suggested that maintaining a wormhole stable enough for people to traverse may require energy conditions unlike those associated with ordinary matter.

In quantum field theory, effects related to negative energy density have been discussed under limited circumstances. Yet no method has been established for constructing and maintaining an immense wormhole network in practice. In the case of wormholes with a time difference between their mouths, quantum effects and gravitational backreaction have also been investigated as possible threats to their stability.

Considering these issues led me to conclude that several rules would be needed to explain how galactic civilization handled wormholes in the novel. I would have to determine whether wormholes had been discovered naturally or manufactured by humans, what kind of energy was needed to maintain them, and under what conditions a passage could close or change its destination. An especially important question was how the existing transportation network would be affected when Noah connected it to the storage space.

Through this examination, wormholes developed from a means of traveling over enormous distances into a device connecting the novel's temporal structure with the energy deficit.

18. If Energy Disappears from Our Universe, What Happens to Energy Conservation?

While examining the connection between the photon deficit and the storage space, I returned to the question of energy conservation. Suppose observers in the galaxy calculated the amount of light emitted by a particular star but found that less light was actually being detected than expected. If they could identify no intervening matter that had absorbed the light or celestial object that had scattered it, they would have to investigate where the missing energy had gone.

Initially, they might consider unknown forms of matter or new physical interactions. Instrumental errors, gravitational lensing, and problems with the propagation of electromagnetic signals would also have to be investigated. But if similar deficits were observed in different regions of the galaxy and their magnitude increased according to a consistent pattern, the phenomenon would become difficult to explain using existing explanations alone.

At this point, the scientists in the novel would begin questioning the scope within which the law of energy conservation should be applied. In general relativity, the local conservation of energy and momentum is an important principle. Defining the total energy of an expanding universe as a single quantity and discussing its conservation, however, is a far more complicated matter. Where the universe lacks a common time-translation symmetry, there are limitations on defining a globally conserved energy quantity.

For the novel, I assumed a situation in which energy no longer observable within the galaxy was moving into another spacetime. The movement of energy could be traced only by considering our universe, the storage space, and the structure connecting them together. This premise changed the way the photon deficit was explained. Initially, I had imagined energy actually disappearing. With the introduction of the storage space, the phenomenon developed into one in which energy moved beyond the observable region.

19. What Kind of Energy Does the Scientist of the Future Collect?

As I worked out the connection between the storage space and the galaxy, I designed the collection process in stages. Initially, the scientist collects photons emitted by stars. Observers discover that less starlight is arriving than expected and begin investigating the cause. At first, they might attribute the phenomenon to problems with their instruments or methods of observation.

I subsequently considered collecting energy from stars, interstellar matter, and other celestial bodies. As the energy recovery process expands, the distribution of matter within the galaxy and the motions of celestial bodies could also change. The abnormal depletion of energy stored in black holes could be placed in the later stages of these developments.

In the final stage, the scientist prepares the initial conditions necessary to start a new universe. What matters here is not only the total quantity of energy and matter but also how they are arranged.

If the scientist is assumed to be capable of expanding the storage space sufficiently and controlling its internal conditions, it becomes possible to imagine an independent universe beginning to expand within it. The Big Bang is better understood not as an explosion of matter within pre-existing space, but in relation to the history of a universe in which space itself expanded from an early hot, dense state. Accordingly, rather than depicting a new universe exploding within an existing space, I devised a structure in which a separate spacetime begins expanding according to its own initial conditions. By this stage, the purpose of the storage space had developed from the long-term preservation of energy into the formation of a new universe.

20. Can Life Reappear in a New Universe?

Having assumed that a new universe could form, I examined the possibility of life emerging within it. Even if a universe contained sufficient energy and matter, that would not necessarily guarantee the formation of complex chemical structures. Stable elementary particles and atoms, diverse chemical bonds, long-lasting energy sources, and environments capable of supporting material cycles and evolution might all be necessary.

Nor could it be known whether the physical constants of the new universe would be the same as those of the existing one. Stable atoms might never form, or stars might not survive long enough. There was therefore insufficient basis for assuming that life would necessarily emerge simply because the future scientist had created a new universe. This examination led me to reconsider the scientist's purpose. If the scientist wanted humanity to survive, preserving human DNA, memories, or the records of civilization would be possible alternatives. In an era of highly advanced galactic civilization, it would be reasonable to imagine technologies capable of preserving biological information and growing new bodies.

But the choice would be different if the scientist had no interest in preserving humanity as a species. Rather than restoring particular life forms, the scientist might attempt to establish the physical conditions under which life could arise again in a new universe. This premise raised an ethical question about the scientist's actions. The possibility of life emerging in the new universe was uncertain, while the existing galaxy, from which energy was being collected, was home to living beings who actually existed.

21. How Can Present Life Be Compared with Future Possibility?

The central question now shifted from physics to ethics. The scientist of the distant future concludes that the energy resources of the existing universe are steadily diminishing and that the chances of civilization's survival are also declining. Rather than restoring the existing civilization, the scientist chooses to establish conditions under which life might emerge in a new universe.

Here, I needed to consider what kind of person the scientist would be. If the character acted for personal survival or fame, the relationship between the scientist's ends and means would be relatively easy to explain. But if the scientist pursued the birth of a new universe while accepting the scientist's own death, the motivation would be different. The scientist compares the time that living beings in the present universe have left with the possibility of future life that does not yet exist. Believing that the present universe will eventually die, the scientist may consider it more valuable to secure the possibility of life emerging in a new universe.

Yet such a judgment contains assumptions that cannot be verified. Whether the new universe can actually be created, whether life will emerge there, and how long that life might endure are all unknown. The decision therefore gives rise to a profound ethical conflict. The scientist believes the action is being taken for the sake of future life, but the choice destroys the conditions necessary for the survival of countless beings who already exist.

Considering this problem led me to take an interest in how a person acting from benevolent intentions might come to determine the lives of others. This question also became connected to the political and social conflicts appearing throughout the novel.

22. How Does Galactic Civilization Understand Its Own Extinction?

After examining the physical premises, I turned my attention to how the inhabitants of galactic civilization might respond to these phenomena. Initially, they could conclude that the civilization's own energy consumption was responsible for the problem. If they believed that they had shortened the lifetimes of stars and extracted too much interstellar matter, they would introduce policies restricting energy use and conserving resources. Such policies would be based on the expectation that the civilization could solve the problem through its own actions.

Yet once the photon deficit was discovered and its cause proved difficult to explain, other interpretations might emerge. People could consider unknown natural phenomena, intervention by an extraterrestrial civilization, or changes that could not be explained by existing physics. Some would believe that they themselves were responsible for the problem, while others would regard it as a natural change for which they bore no responsibility. Disputes over scientific explanations could become entangled with questions of political responsibility.

If Noah's actions and the structure of the storage space were revealed in the distant future, these judgments would change again. The possibility would emerge that the decline of the existing civilization was connected to the creation of a new universe in the future. This premise led me to consider how a civilization's understanding of a crisis, and its response to that crisis, could be portrayed across different historical periods. The same phenomenon can be interpreted in entirely different ways depending on the era and the information available to those living in it.

23. In What Chronological Order Should the Events Be Arranged?

As I organized the scientific premises, I considered dividing the novel's temporal structure into three stages.

The first is the era in which humanity settles in different regions of the galaxy and develops the wormhole network. Civilization grows by connecting travel and communication between star systems and making use of enormous amounts of energy. The infrastructure that will later be connected to the storage space is established during this era.

The second is the era in which the photon deficit is discovered in different regions of the galaxy and civilization begins investigating its cause. What is initially regarded as an observational problem gradually develops into a crisis encompassing the entire galaxy. People propose various hypotheses, including natural phenomena, new physical laws, intervention by an extraterrestrial civilization, and environmental changes caused by energy consumption.

The third takes place in the far more distant future, when Noah appears. Most of the galaxy's energy resources have been exhausted and human civilization is approaching extinction. Noah prepares the initial conditions of a new universe and undertakes the task of connecting the storage space to the wormhole network that has existed since the distant past.

In connecting these three eras, I became interested in the possibility that the chronological order of events and the order in which their causes are understood might be different. Readers first encounter the photon deficit in the galaxy of the past. The era in which its cause can be understood lies much further in the future. People in the past act according to their own records and observations, while those in the future make judgments with more information about earlier events.

This structure also explains why it would be difficult for a single character to narrate the history of an entire civilization from beginning to end. Observers and records from each era interpret events within the limits of what they know. These differences also influenced my decision to construct the story through the forms of a dictionary and documentary records.

24. Which Parts Are Physics, and Which Are Assumptions Made for the Novel?

As the thought experiment became more complex, it was necessary to distinguish between established physical knowledge and the additional assumptions introduced for the novel. The fact that photons possess energy and momentum, that light energy contributes to gravity, and that the availability of energy depends on thermodynamic conditions are all grounded in established physics. The treatment of time and simultaneity in relativity, the mass and rotational energy of black holes, and observations relating to the expansion of the universe also provided a foundation.

Wormholes and baby universes presented a different problem. General relativity and quantum gravity research examine relevant mathematical models and theoretical possibilities. Yet traversable wormholes and devices capable of creating new universes for energy storage are not technologies whose existence has been confirmed by observation.

For the novel, several additional assumptions were necessary. These included the existence of a wormhole network connecting the entire galaxy, the ability of a scientist in the distant future to alter its connections, and the possibility of transferring energy and matter into a new spacetime to establish the initial conditions of an independent universe. In particular, the structure through which an action in the future affects the wormhole network of the past belongs to the technological possibilities invented for this novel. Even if a self-consistent temporal structure is adopted, whether such technology could actually exist remains a separate question.

Making these distinctions helped develop the premises. Where established physical laws could be applied, I examined the conditions they imposed. Where explanations proved difficult, I identified the assumptions needed to make the story work.

25. How Was the Novel's Form Determined?

As the scientific premises developed, I also began considering the form in which the story should be told. A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction encompasses immense spans of time, multiple civilizations, and characters living in different eras. A scientist in the era when the photon deficit is discovered cannot know what Noah will do in the distant future. Governments, the media, civic organizations, and religious groups interpret the same events from different perspectives. I thought it might be difficult to address events scattered across such vast stretches of time and space by following the experiences of a single individual.

Works by Borges and Milorad Pavić, which I had read long ago, influenced my thinking about this problem. I was interested in forms that allowed readers to construct a world through documents, records, and separate entries. I therefore decided to structure the novel as a dictionary. Its entries would present definitions of individual events, records of people, scientific concepts, administrative institutions, and the history of civilization. As readers move through these entries, they can discover relationships between events and compare interpretations that change over time.

This form was also connected to the development of the scientific thought experiments. Just as one question leads to another, one entry can provide information necessary to understand another. In this way, the structure of the thought experiments also influenced the novel's composition.

26. How Far Did the Question That Began with a Candle Lead?

Returning to the original question, it began with a comparison between a candle burning in an ordinary room and one burning in a room lined with mirrors. The thought experiment began with the realization that even when the same amount of light is emitted, the amount of energy present in a room can differ depending on how long that light remains inside it. I then considered whether light could be confined perfectly. I examined the properties of photons and the conservation of energy, along with the problems of gravity and pressure that arise when a large amount of energy is concentrated in a narrow space. The idea of containing enormous energy inside a small sphere developed into the premise of an entrance connected to another spacetime.

Thinking about the new storage space led me to questions about its internal time, its energy states, and the possibility of a separate universe forming within it. The proposed contents of the storage space also expanded, from light to matter and black holes, and eventually to the initial conditions required to start a new universe. I subsequently examined how a scientist in the future might collect energy from the galaxy of the past. The idea of installing new devices in the past was replaced by one that made use of an existing wormhole network, and Noah's role was revised accordingly. After examining the paradoxes of time travel, I organized the premise around a self-consistent history.

Finally, I considered whether life could emerge in a new universe, and how to understand the sacrifice of living beings in the present for the possibility of future life. A thought experiment that began with a question about physics had led to questions concerning human choices and the fate of civilization.

Of the various hypotheses examined during this process, some were incorporated into the novel, while others were revised or discarded. The remaining premises do not all represent established scientific facts. The world of A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction was constructed from these questions and assumptions. The finished novel did not need to contain every stage of the thought experiments. Its characters act according to the conditions of the world they inhabit, understanding the causes and consequences of events in their own ways. These notes preserve a record of the questions that arose as that world was being created, and of how its conception changed as those questions were examined.
