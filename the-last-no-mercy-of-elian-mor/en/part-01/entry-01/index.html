Part I — The First Slow Ship

# Heating

The first slow ship maintained a central thermal management network throughout its voyage of approximately 1,200 years. The ship’s heating facilities had been installed before departure and were designed to supply the temperatures required by residential areas, food production facilities, medical facilities, educational facilities, public facilities, machinery rooms, connecting passages, and other areas. Each compartment had a separate heat-exchange network, all under the control of the central thermal management system. Temperatures and heat supply were adjusted according to the purpose of each facility, the number of people using it, and its operating condition.

The ship’s original capacity was set at 3,500 humans and 3,500 robots. The human population consisted of 3,000 general passengers and 500 professional crew members. The robots were manufactured in various forms, ranging from small inspection robots to humanoids, and each was assigned a specialized field and area of responsibility. Humans and robots held equal status as persons and members of the ship. Each member was granted authority and responsibility according to their duties, and unauthorized damage to another member’s body or functional systems was prohibited. The robots could make decisions and perform tasks independently. Work instructions, regular software updates, and common operational information were transmitted through the ship’s communications network. Even when communications were interrupted, the robots could continue their work using their individual storage devices and decision-making systems.

The ship’s specialized operations were divided into eight fields: navigation and propulsion, energy and machinery, food production and ecology, atmosphere and water resources, medicine and public health, education and society, administration and population, and military affairs and security. The energy and machinery division was responsible for power generation facilities, electricity supply, heating and cooling, thermal circulation, and the maintenance of mechanical equipment. Atmosphere and water resources managed air circulation, water recovery, drinking water supply, and the removal of pollutants. The two fields shared information concerning heat transfer and air circulation and jointly investigated the effects of compartment closures or changes in supply levels on other life-support facilities. Human technicians and robots worked together to repair thermal management facilities. Small robots inspected the interiors of pipes and areas that were difficult to access, while large work robots participated in component replacement, the movement of machinery, and the installation of maintenance facilities. Human technicians prepared work plans and coordinated schedules with the personnel responsible for other facilities. Each task had specified qualifications and access permissions.

Heat was continuously generated inside the ship by power generation facilities, propulsion systems, machinery, and the activities of humans and robots. The thermal management facilities recovered the heat produced and directed it to where it was needed, while excess heat was released into space through external radiators. Changes in the output of power generation facilities and the operating rates of machinery also changed the amount of heat produced. In agricultural facilities, temperature and humidity were adjusted according to crop species and stages of growth. Medical facilities maintained the environments required by patients’ conditions and treatments. Temperatures in residential areas were managed according to the number of residents and their daily schedules.

The central thermal management network included a primary circulation network and a backup circulation network. When an abnormality was detected in the primary network, the automatic control system isolated the affected section and supplied heat through the backup network. Several compartments sometimes shared a single backup heat source. Each compartment was equipped with independent control and shutoff devices, designed to maintain the operation of unaffected areas during an accident. The ship had been constructed by joining multiple modules. Each module contained facilities necessary for survival and equipment for independent operation. If maintaining the safety of the entire ship became difficult, certain modules could be isolated or the hull could be separated into several sections. Emergency plans prepared during the design stage also allowed the ship to be divided into three or five independent ships capable of continuing their voyages. The piping and control systems of the thermal management facilities were installed with these separation functions in mind.

After the first slow ship left the solar system and entered interstellar space, several problems arose in its thermal management facilities. In some compartments, differences appeared between the heat demand anticipated during design and actual consumption. Changes in the operating condition of power generation facilities or increases in the number of people using particular areas required adjustments to the heat supply. The operating hours of residential and public facilities were also changed several times after departure. During early maintenance operations, technicians adjusted the flow rates of the heat-exchange networks, modified temperature control devices, and revised pipe inspection intervals. Reduced circulation efficiency was observed in some pipes. Technicians replaced damaged components and adjusted heat supply routes. These operations were carried out within limits that would not affect other facilities in normal operation.

Differences in heat consumption among compartments remained an issue throughout the voyage. When the number of residents in a particular compartment decreased because of relocation, the supply could be reduced. Additional heat was required when medical facilities experienced increased demand or agricultural facilities began cultivating new crops. Maintenance personnel investigated the requirements of each facility and revised supply plans. As generations passed after departure, the technicians who had participated in the ship’s original design and construction gradually died. Later generations of technicians used design drawings, maintenance histories, and investigation reports from past accidents in their training. The structure of the heat-exchange networks, the locations of pipes, component replacement intervals, and emergency operating procedures were included in specialized training courses. New technicians received training in both actual facilities and simulated environments.

Operating regulations were also revised several times. The early regulations focused on procedures for individual equipment failures and repairs. Later, additional regulations were introduced to address situations in which problems occurred simultaneously in several compartments. Standards for using backup circulation networks, priorities for heat supply among compartments, temperatures required for emergency evacuation, and procedures for isolating facilities during repairs were established. The long-term operation of thermal management facilities required the production and recycling of components. The ship’s manufacturing facilities produced or repaired pipes, connectors, control devices, and components used in heat exchangers. When major equipment had to be replaced, the required materials, energy, and manufacturing time were calculated. Components that remained usable were employed in repairs to other facilities.

From the time of departure, the ship maintained survival reserves for prolonged emergencies. Emergency food for humans was stored in the form of highly concentrated capsules supplying the calories and nutrients required for one day. The standard quantity required to feed 3,500 humans for one year was 1,277,500 capsules. This figure was calculated on the basis of one capsule per person per day and a year of 365 days. Emergency food was distributed among eight major sections, each with an independent inventory management system. Reserve energy storage devices were also prepared separately for the robots. A compact storage device manufactured using the technology available at departure could supply enough energy for approximately one year of activity by a single humanoid. The ship carried the minimum number of reserve storage devices required to provide one to each of its 3,500 robots. Under normal conditions, the robots used the ship’s electricity supply facilities. They could use individual storage devices when independent activity was required. The quantity of emergency reserves was taken into account in the operating plans for the food production facilities. If agricultural operations ceased, the remaining food supply and the time required to restore production had to be calculated. Crop cultivation periods, seed preservation conditions, and the possibility of restoring production facilities were also considered. Each section regularly inspected its stocks of emergency food and energy storage devices.

Routine decisions concerning the thermal management facilities were made within energy and machinery. Major matters affecting several life-support facilities were deliberated by Cloud Nine. Cloud Nine was the highest decision-making body, consisting of eight human members representing the specialized fields and the AI Smith. Each of the nine members had one vote. The captain could attend the committee’s deliberations and was responsible for deciding whether to execute its resolutions and for bearing command responsibility for the consequences. Cloud Nine regularly reviewed the condition of the power generation facilities and thermal management network. Heat consumption by compartment, the operating rates of backup facilities, component replacement plans, and long-term energy requirements were among its principal subjects of deliberation. When the supply to a particular facility had to be changed, members representing the relevant fields submitted the necessary information.

Most heating problems that arose during the early voyage were resolved through on-site maintenance and changes to operating procedures. Some failures were overcome by the activation of backup circulation networks, while others required pipe replacement or modifications to control devices. Technicians applied their experience from previous repairs to the manufacture and operation of new equipment. Through these improvements, the central thermal management network remained in use for several hundred years. A major heating incident occurred during the tenure of the 14th captain, Amelia Dorn. Approximately 370 years had passed since departure. By then, the central thermal management network had undergone maintenance and improvements over many generations, and operating regulations established through previous accidents were in effect throughout the compartments. The incident began when the flow rate of the circulating fluid decreased in part of the central heat-exchange network. The automatic control system detected the abnormality and activated the backup circulation network. Temperatures in several compartments subsequently moved outside the permitted range. Food production facilities, residential areas, and medical facilities were connected to the same backup heat source, making it necessary to redistribute heat in order to maintain normal operations.

Personnel in energy and machinery prepared a plan to isolate the affected piping and maintain the supply to unaffected compartments. Robots measured temperatures and circulation flow rates in each compartment. The components and working time required for repairs were investigated, and preparations were made to evacuate residents from areas where heat supply could not be maintained. Cloud Nine convened an emergency meeting. The energy and machinery member reported on the condition of the thermal management network and the resources required for repairs. The food production and ecology member explained the potential damage to crops and production equipment if temperatures in the agricultural facilities fell. The medicine and public health member presented information on the condition of hospital patients and the health problems that could arise in low-temperature environments. The administration and population member investigated the number of residents in each compartment and the capacity of facilities available for evacuation.

A shutdown of the food production facilities could also affect the ship’s long-term food supply. At the time, emergency food for the 3,500 humans was maintained on the basis of a maximum supply of one year. If restoring normal agricultural production required a prolonged period, both the consumption of emergency food and the remaining quantity had to be calculated. Some crops and production equipment could require considerable time to recover if temperatures fell below certain limits. The meeting considered the possibility of restoring agricultural facilities, the remaining reserve power, and the time required to evacuate residents. Because several facilities shared the same heat source, adjusting the supply to one facility also affected temperatures in other compartments. Some residential areas could no longer maintain normal temperatures, and their residents had to be moved elsewhere. The connecting passages used for evacuation were also affected by the central thermal management network. Maintaining the temperatures of those passages required a certain level of heat supply.

Smith preserved two principal heat-allocation plans submitted to the captain. One plan prioritized maintaining the heat supply required by the food production facilities. The other included measures to maintain the temperatures of the evacuation passages at the cost of a portion of food production. Both plans were accompanied by data on anticipated temperature changes, residents’ evacuation times, and the possibility of restoring agricultural facilities. Amelia Dorn ordered the heat supply to the food production facilities to be maintained. Under this order, heat supply to some residential areas was cut off. Evacuation orders were issued at the same time. Maintenance personnel changed the heat supply routes, and evacuation personnel began moving the residents. As the evacuation proceeded, temperatures in the passages connecting the compartments fell faster than expected. The movement of residents was delayed in some areas. Patients suffering from hypothermia were transferred to medical facilities, and residents also died as a result of the incident. Records of the patients and fatalities were prepared by the Medical and Public Health Bureau. Repair work proceeded according to procedures for isolating damaged sections and maintaining the supply to unaffected areas. Maintenance personnel in each compartment inspected the backup circulation network and replaced the necessary components. In compartments where heat supply had been restored, temperatures and circulation flow rates were measured.

After the incident, Cloud Nine established an accident investigation committee. The investigation examined the initial mechanical failure separately from the captain’s heat-allocation order. Its subjects included the circumstances in which the flow rate decreased in the central heat-exchange network, the operation of the automatic control system, the performance of the backup circulation network, and the temperature changes in the evacuation passages. The committee compared the two heat-allocation plans provided to Amelia Dorn with the order that was actually executed. It also investigated whether part of the food production facilities could have remained operational while maintaining the minimum temperature required in the evacuation passages. Smith’s records showed that a plan to secure the heat supply to the evacuation passages at the cost of a portion of food production had been submitted before the order was issued.

Cloud Nine determined that Amelia Dorn had failed to fulfill her obligation to adequately review the risk data provided to her. Amelia Dorn was impeached, and Thomas Müller was appointed as her successor. The list of fatalities was preserved by the Medical and Public Health Bureau, and the equipment restoration records by the Energy and Machinery Bureau. Smith included the heat-allocation plans, the emergency meeting deliberations, the captain’s order, and the investigation findings in the voyage records.

Following the heating incident, a simulation involving the operation of multiple life-support facilities during a prolonged energy shortage was added to the qualification examination for captain candidates. Candidates were required to adjust heat supply to residential areas, medical facilities, and agricultural facilities and to calculate the consumption of emergency reserves. The examination also evaluated the effects of each decision on the survival of residents at the time and on food production over the following decades. The heat-allocation data from the incident were subsequently used in captain selection examinations and specialized technical training. Procedures for calculating the temperatures of evacuation passages and residents’ travel times were also improved. Standards for operating multiple life-support facilities simultaneously during prolonged energy shortages were incorporated into later operating regulations. Even after Amelia Dorn’s impeachment, major and minor failures continued to occur in the thermal management facilities. Some resulted from damaged components, while others were associated with aging equipment and replacement work. Maintenance personnel continued replacing pipes, modifying control devices, and making adjustments to improve the efficiency of heat supply in each compartment. As the voyage continued, a substantial portion of the equipment installed at departure was replaced. New components were manufactured, and equipment no longer in use was removed or repurposed. Thermal management plans were revised whenever the operating conditions of the power generation facilities, agricultural facilities, or residential areas changed. Generations of technicians and robots participated in this work.

The ship’s timekeeping system used both an internal standard time and the standard time of the civilization from which it had departed. Internal standard time was used for residents’ daily lives, crew duties, and the operation and maintenance of facilities. The originating civilization’s standard time was used for voyage records and for calculating time differences between the ship and external civilizations. Smith continuously calculated the difference between the two time systems, taking the ship’s velocity and trajectory into account. The times of major incidents were also recorded so that they could be converted between the two standards.

By the time Robert Patterson took office as the 39th captain following the death of the 38th captain, Emil Lowell, the central thermal management network was operating normally. The ship was conducting deceleration operations for its approach to Utopia, and the required heat was being supplied to the food production facilities, residential areas, and medical facilities. Energy and machinery inspected the thermal management facilities in each compartment according to the scheduled maintenance plan. The central heat-exchange network and backup circulation network were operating in accordance with the ship’s voyage plan. Temperatures in the food production facilities and the heat supply to residential areas remained within normal limits. The ship was approaching its destination along the planned course.
