7. Captain

— A Sample Chapter from A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction, Volume II — The Last No Mercy of Elian Mor, Part I: The First Slow Ship

November 16, 2026

The first slow ship had 39 captains during its voyage of nearly 1,200 years after departing Earth. The first captain was Aaron Dupont, and the 39th was Robert Patterson. Two captains were impeached, and several died while in office. The longest-serving captain was the 17th, Jonas Field, who held the position for 64 years and 8 months. The sixth captain, Samir Khan, died 19 days after taking office. His successor served as acting captain for 47 days until the procedures required for formal appointment were completed. Even during periods when the captain's position was vacant, the ship continued along its planned course.

The average term of office was approximately 31 years. There was no fixed term limit, and a captain could remain in office as long as they retained their qualifications in military affairs and security and were capable of performing their duties. The appointment and removal of captains, as well as the designation of acting captains, required the approval of Cloud Nine. The practice of a serving captain nominating a successor had been maintained since the ship's departure. The nominated candidate underwent a review by Cloud Nine, and if approval was not obtained, another candidate had to be submitted.

The ship's original designers referred to the captain as the tenth person. Cloud Nine consisted of eight human members representing navigation and propulsion, energy and machinery, food production and ecology, atmosphere and water resources, medicine and public health, education and society, administration and population, and military affairs and security, together with the AI Smith. Each of the nine members had one vote. Major decisions, including changes of course, replacement of power generation facilities, long-term resource allocation, closure of compartments, and forced relocation of residents, were deliberated by Cloud Nine. The captain could attend committee meetings but did not participate in voting. The captain decided whether to execute the resolutions and bore command responsibility for their consequences.

The inauguration record of the first captain, Aaron Dupont, contained the following provision. The Tenth Person Principle. If all nine members of Cloud Nine reached the same conclusion, the captain was required to oppose that conclusion unconditionally. The opposing opinion had to be submitted as an official record, and the matter had to undergo reconsideration. This procedure applied equally to proposals approved unanimously and proposals rejected unanimously. The application of the Tenth Person Principle did not require the captain to hold a personal conviction or possess specialized knowledge concerning the matter. The captain had to prepare an opposing opinion even on a proposal they supported. The matters to be examined included conditions excluded from the original deliberation, minority possibilities, uncertainties in predictive data, the possibility of reversing the decision, and the effects an incorrect decision might have on the ship's future voyage. The original conclusion could be maintained after reconsideration. The restriction applied only to the use of the initial unanimous vote as grounds for execution.

The Tenth Person Principle was first invoked in the eleventh year after departure. Cloud Nine unanimously decided to close a large maintenance facility that was no longer in use and employ its materials to manufacture spare parts for the propulsion system. The captain at the time submitted an opinion opposing the facility's closure. During reconsideration, the energy and manufacturing time required to restore a maintenance space of the same size after dismantling the facility were examined. Cloud Nine withdrew its original decision. The maintenance facility remained in use for more than 600 years afterward. Conversely, during the tenure of the 23rd captain, Matthias Paul, a reconsideration initiated under the Tenth Person Principle continued for 18 months. At that time, Cloud Nine had unanimously decided to close an aging seed preservation facility. Following the captain's objection, the possibility of genetically restoring several hundred species of seeds was investigated again. The original closure decision was upheld after reconsideration. The electricity and labor used to operate the facility during those 18 months were recorded as separate maintenance costs.

The captain's veto power also applied to resolutions that were not unanimous. If the captain suspended the execution of a proposal approved by a majority of Cloud Nine, the committee was required to examine the reasons for the veto and the relevant materials. Records of a captain's exercise of veto power included the time the order was suspended, the duration of the suspension, the safety regulations applied, and the outcome of reconsideration. The captain also bore official responsibility for any damage caused by the exercise of veto power. Military facilities under the captain's command were distributed across several sections of the ship. In addition to defensive devices designed to prevent collisions with external celestial bodies, the first slow ship carried weapons capable of destroying objects attacking the ship. The weapons systems had been built using the technology available at the time of departure and underwent maintenance and performance testing throughout the voyage. The military affairs and security member assessed the technical condition of the weapons, while the captain had the authority to order their use. A separately maintained military authentication procedure was used to authorize the activation of the weapons.

Most recorded instances of the ship's actual use of weapons involved internal armed disturbances. The first impeachment of a captain occurred after communications with Earth were lost. During the early years of the voyage, regular communications were maintained between Earth and the ship. The time required for a signal sent from Earth to reach the ship and for the ship's reply to reach Earth increased as the distance grew. Communications personnel calculated transmission and reception times and preserved the correspondence. After the last normal reply was confirmed, no further signals arrived from Earth. The ship continued its voyage while the possibility of a communications equipment failure was investigated.

When it was determined that contact with Earth could not be restored, Cloud Nine examined the possibility of returning. The navigation and propulsion member reported the propellant required for deceleration and a change of direction, the time needed for the return voyage, and the estimated fuel remaining upon arrival at Earth. Smith registered the inability to confirm conditions on Earth as a separate risk factor. The risks of continuing the voyage were also investigated. Before Cloud Nine had completed its deliberations, the third captain, Lee Jun-woo, issued emergency orders to the military and security facilities. He restricted gatherings of passengers demanding a return to Earth and controlled access to major communications facilities. An investigation found that some of these orders exceeded the scope of previously authorized emergency measures.

Cloud Nine suspended the captain's exercise of command authority and initiated impeachment proceedings. The issues examined during the proceedings were the captain's position on returning to Earth and the legality of the orders issued to the military facilities. The captain stated in his defense that the measures had been necessary for the ship's navigational safety. The investigation records included both the time at which the order restricting gatherings was issued and the time at which a direct threat to the facilities was confirmed. The third captain, Lee Jun-woo, was impeached. The fourth captain, Suzuki Haruto, assumed command. Deliberations concerning the return to Earth continued after the new captain took office. The rebellion that subsequently occurred was suppressed through new military orders and judicial procedures. The first slow ship maintained its course toward its destination.

The second impeachment was recorded as the heating incident. During the tenure of the 14th captain, Amelia Dorn, the flow rate of the circulating fluid decreased in part of the central heat-exchange network. The automatic control system activated a backup circulation network, but temperatures in several compartments moved outside the permitted range. Food production facilities, residential areas, and medical facilities were connected to the same backup heat source. The energy and machinery member submitted a proposal to isolate the affected piping and redistribute heat among the compartments. At an emergency meeting of Cloud Nine, the population of each compartment, the condition of hospital patients, the possibility of restoring agricultural facilities, and the remaining reserve power were examined. Some compartments could no longer maintain their temperatures, requiring residents to be moved elsewhere. The passages used for evacuation were also affected by the heating network. The captain at the time ordered heat supply to the food production facilities to be maintained. As a result of this order, heat supply to some residential areas was cut off. Evacuation orders were issued at the same time, but temperatures in the passages connecting the compartments fell faster than expected. The movement of residents was delayed, and patients suffering from hypothermia were transferred to medical facilities.

The accident investigation committee examined the initial mechanical failure separately from the captain's heat-allocation order. The investigation considered whether part of the food production facilities could have remained operational while maintaining the minimum temperature required in the evacuation passages. Smith's records contained two allocation plans submitted before the captain issued the order. One of them proposed maintaining the heat supply to the evacuation passages at the cost of a portion of food production. Cloud Nine determined that the captain had failed to fulfill her obligation to examine the risk data provided. The 14th captain was impeached, and Thomas Müller was appointed as her successor. The list of fatalities and the equipment restoration records relating to the heating incident were preserved by the Medical and Public Health Bureau and the Energy and Machinery Bureau, respectively. Afterward, the qualification examination for captain candidates included a simulation involving the operation of multiple life-support facilities during a prolonged energy shortage. The examination lasted for an extended period, and candidates were evaluated on the effects their decisions would have on food production several decades later. The heat-allocation data used during the heating incident remained among the source materials for the examination.

The procedure for appointing captains was revised several times after departure. Beginning with the 18th captain, a regulation came into effect requiring at least six votes from Cloud Nine to approve a candidate nominated by the serving captain. Candidates were required to retain their qualifications in military affairs and security and to pass an emergency command examination and an oral examination conducted by specialist members. If Cloud Nine rejected a candidate, the serving captain nominated another. Even a captain who had met all the qualifications at the time of appointment was required to designate an acting captain if illness or an accident later made it impossible to perform their duties. A captain's loss of capacity to perform official duties was determined through a review by the medical and public health member and deliberation by Cloud Nine. The acting captain was granted command authority necessary to maintain the ship's safety. Some of the powers granted to a formally appointed captain required separate approval.

The 38th captain, Emil Lowell, ceased performing his duties while the first slow ship was reviewing its deceleration plan ahead of its scheduled arrival at the destination planet. The planet's official designation was Alpha 157, but aboard the ship it was commonly known as Utopia. Emil Lowell continued to perform limited duties after beginning treatment at a medical facility. The medical and public health member regularly assessed his treatment progress and command capacity. As the periods during which Lowell remained conscious became shorter, Cloud Nine approved the transition to an acting captain system. Robert Patterson was designated as acting captain. Patterson had served in military affairs and security before his formal appointment and had already been nominated as Lowell's successor. He had also completed the necessary procedures for Cloud Nine's selection review. When the decision concerning the acting captain was registered, Smith updated the ship's command system. The authorization systems in the captain's office and the military facilities were updated with the authentication information of the new acting captain.

The period of acting command lasted approximately three months. During this time, Cloud Nine deliberated several times on the deceleration plan required for the approach to Utopia. The navigation and propulsion member reported the remaining propellant and the operating time of the deceleration engines. The military affairs and security member submitted data on artificial structures observed near the destination. When the first slow ship departed, no human-made facilities existed on Utopia. Because contact with Earth had been lost during the voyage, the ship had no updated official information concerning the facilities constructed at its destination. Patterson attended meetings concerning the deceleration plan and external communications procedures. Opinions he submitted in his capacity as acting captain were recorded separately from orders issued by a formally appointed captain.

Emil Lowell's death was included in a regular report from the Medical and Public Health Bureau during the voyage. The medical and public health member confirmed his death and notified Cloud Nine. Smith closed the service record of the 38th captain and registered the time of death. Patterson retained his status as acting captain until the formal appointment procedure was completed. It was decided that the funeral of the 38th captain would be held at the central memorial facility. The funeral schedule was arranged in consideration of the ship's deceleration operations and major equipment inspection periods. All eight human members of Cloud Nine and Smith were designated to participate in the funeral. Separate documents were prepared concerning the captain's death and the succession to his position.

Robert Patterson's formal appointment was approved before the funeral took place. At Cloud Nine's appointment meeting, Patterson's qualifications in military affairs and security, his command record during the acting period, his health, and the record of his nomination as successor were verified. The vote resulted in eight in favor and one against. The dissenting vote came from the navigation and propulsion member. He proposed maintaining the acting captain system until the approach to Utopia and the deceleration operations had been completed. The majority of members determined that a situation requiring the authority of a formally appointed captain was approaching. Smith recorded the voting result and the minority opinion and prepared the appointment certificate. Patterson reviewed the regulations concerning the powers and duties of the captain's office and signed the document. From the moment his signature was registered, the military authentication system was updated to accept orders from the 39th captain.

No personal belongings of former captains were preserved in the captain's office. Official orders and meeting records were stored in Smith and in independent preservation facilities, while personal belongings were returned to the individuals or their families upon retirement. What was transferred to the 39th captain consisted of command authority, pending matters, ongoing military orders, emergency plans for the deceleration period, and the obligation to nominate the next successor. Robert Patterson's first formal order approved the existing plan for the funeral of the 38th captain, Emil Lowell. His second order was to maintain the operational status of the defensive systems during the approach to the destination. His third order concerned the preparation of the final voyage report to be submitted to Cloud Nine.

On the day after the 39th captain took office, Smith recalculated the average term of office of the captains. The term of the 38th captain was finalized, and the term of the 39th captain was recorded as one day. The register of former captains was updated using the format that had been maintained since the ship's departure from Earth. The ship's navigation records contained the remaining distance to the destination and the estimated arrival date. The deceleration engines were operating according to schedule, and the central thermal management network and food production facilities were also functioning normally. Cloud Nine registered the procedures for approaching Utopia, communications with external artificial facilities, and preparations for the residents' landing as agenda items for its next regular meeting. The record concerning the appointment of Robert Patterson as the 39th captain was closed as the first item of that meeting.
