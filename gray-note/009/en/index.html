The Remaining Lego Pieces

— On Hearing That an Anthropic Mathematician Had Proved the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude

November 24, 2026

A few days ago, I came across news about a study that attempted to prove the Riemann hypothesis using artificial intelligence. Proposed in 1859 by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, the hypothesis is said to have occupied countless mathematicians for more than 160 years. I wondered what changes a proof of the Riemann hypothesis might bring to human life. Perhaps I would notice no change at all during my lifetime. Riemann studied the distribution of prime numbers through a mathematical function called the zeta function and conjectured that certain zeros of this function lie on a line in the complex plane where the real part is one-half. I am not a mathematician, so I do not really understand what this means. While reading explanations of the Riemann hypothesis, I found myself imagining a children's ball pit.

In a children's playroom, thousands of balls about the size of a fist lie scattered around. Blue, yellow, orange, and green balls are mixed together. Imagine that the children have spent the whole day playing with them and have gone home. There are fewer green balls than balls of any other colour. Someone records the positions of the green balls, which the children have thrown about at random, one by one, and examines the distances between them. In doing so, they discover a mathematical order in their distribution. When I imagined the prime numbers scattered throughout the natural numbers as those green balls, something like that picture formed in my mind.

For many years, I subscribed to the Korean edition of the Japanese science magazine Newton. I also have a few books on physics and mathematics at home. I enjoy watching science documentaries produced by the BBC, NHK, and the American broadcaster PBS. There are many times when I do not properly understand what they are explaining, but I find them interesting. As I read these books and watch documentaries, something I heard somewhere years ago will occasionally come back to me. When I heard the news about the Riemann hypothesis, I remembered a story about two people I had learned about long ago: the British mathematician Hugh Montgomery and the physicist Freeman Dyson. The two met in Princeton, in the United States, in 1972. Montgomery was studying the intervals between the zeros of the function Riemann had investigated. When Dyson saw an equation that appeared in Montgomery's work, he recognised the same mathematical form from studies of the distribution of energy levels in quantum physics. How could someone studying a mathematical problem related to the distribution of prime numbers and someone studying the physical world discover the same mathematical structure? I have wondered about that ever since I first heard the story.

Another story that has stayed with me concerns the Italian physicist Gabriele Veneziano. In 1968, while studying the forces acting between particles, he used Euler's beta function, which had been developed about 200 years earlier. His research later became one of the starting points of string theory. Many years ago, I watched a documentary called The Elegant Universe, produced by the American broadcaster PBS. It was a three-part series in which the physicist Brian Greene explained superstring theory. There is a scene in the documentary where Veneziano talks about his research. I remember him expressing dissatisfaction with the way his work was described as if he had happened to stumble upon an old mathematical formula. He explained that he had spent a long time researching before he found that formula. Whenever I watch that scene, I find myself wanting to take Veneziano's side.

On reflection, though, perhaps it does not matter so much whether he came across Euler's formula by chance or found it after years of research. The history of science contains many stories of accidental discoveries. What matters may be the ability to recognise the significance of something found by chance and the judgement to use it in one's own research. Euler's beta function had existed for a long time. Countless mathematicians and physicists must have had opportunities to encounter it. Veneziano recognised that the formula could be used to explain a problem he was studying in physics, and he published his findings in a paper. He must have believed his judgement was sound when he presented the work as his own research. It is easy, looking back at someone else's work, to describe it as a chance discovery. I am more curious about the time Veneziano spent working out what he could do with that formula and developing his research than about the moment he first found it.

Thinking about these stories, I was reminded of Lego. Imagine opening a box containing a thousand pieces. There are no instructions. If you spread the pieces across the floor, you can make a rough guess at what they are meant to build. Some pieces are for walls, others for staircases and windows. As you begin putting together the familiar pieces, the shape of a castle gradually emerges. After working on it for quite some time, you find that thirty or forty pieces are still lying on the floor. Among them are some oddly shaped pieces that you cannot work out where to use, no matter how hard you think.

Could the many mathematical formulas and laws of physics humanity has discovered be compared to these Lego pieces? One plus one equals two, and four divided by two equals two. If you let go of something you are holding, it falls to the ground. We understand such things and use them in everyday life. Newton's law of gravity and Einstein's theory of relativity would also be among the pieces humanity has discovered and assembled over a long period of time. And there are still pieces lying on the floor whose purpose remains unknown.

Thousands of years ago, people living in Sumer and Babylonia also observed the night sky. People in the Americas and East Asia recorded the positions of stars and observed how they changed with the seasons. Some of their discoveries were used in agriculture, while others became associated with religion and mythology and continued to influence people's lives for centuries. In the seventeenth century, Galileo used a telescope to observe moons orbiting Jupiter. Newton explained why an apple falls to the ground and why the Moon orbits the Earth through a single law. More than 200 years later, Einstein explained gravity in relation to the structure of spacetime. In the twentieth century, observations by Edwin Hubble helped humanity realise that other galaxies existed beyond the Milky Way.

For thousands of years, humans have discovered new pieces and studied where and how to use the pieces already found. The time required to discover a piece and the time required to understand its purpose have not always been the same. It took about 200 years for a mathematical function developed by Euler to appear in particle physics, and more than a century for an intriguing relationship to be discovered between Riemann's research and quantum physics.

Recently, I have been following debates among researchers about the accelerating expansion of the universe. Depending on how the brightness of supernovae is interpreted, existing explanations of the universe's expansion may have to change. Not long ago, I also watched a video introducing research on spacetime foam involving scientists at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in the United States. The idea was that if spacetime is constantly fluctuating on an extremely small scale, traces of those fluctuations might remain in light that has travelled through the universe for a very long time. I wonder how these studies will develop. Problems currently being investigated in different fields may someday be connected by a single mathematical formula. Someone might discover where one of those strangely shaped Lego pieces lying on the floor belongs.

In the film The Lord of the Rings, Frodo sets out on a long journey with a sword called Sting and a light coat of chain mail made of mithril, both given to him by Bilbo. Frodo does not know where, in the course of his journey, that coat will save his life. I sometimes think that the many mathematical formulas humanity has discovered resemble the objects Frodo carries with him. Someone may spend an entire lifetime discovering a short equation that can be written on paper with just a few letters and numbers. Centuries later, another person may use that equation to solve a problem in an entirely different field. When Maxwell was studying electromagnetism in the nineteenth century, could he have imagined people today carrying palm-sized devices and speaking to someone on the other side of the Earth?

If the Riemann hypothesis is proved, humanity will continue its journey carrying one more object. I wonder when and where that object will be used. I also wonder which part of the castle we have already built might need to be altered when a new piece is discovered. Reading about the lives of scientists, I have learned that this process has not always gone smoothly.

In his younger years, Newton was involved in a long dispute with Robert Hooke over research achievements and priority of discovery. He is said to have been extremely sensitive to the possibility of others taking credit for his work. Later, after becoming president of the Royal Society, Newton became embroiled in a dispute with Leibniz over who had first discovered calculus. Newton himself was deeply involved in the preparation of the Royal Society's investigative report on the dispute.

I also find the story of the British astrophysicist Arthur Eddington rather sad. In 1919, he travelled to Príncipe, an island off the west coast of Africa, to observe a total solar eclipse. His purpose was to test a prediction made by Einstein's general theory of relativity. I find it remarkable that, so soon after the end of the First World War, a British scientist travelled all the way to Africa to test the theory of a German scientist. Yet Eddington later publicly rejected the research of a young Indian physicist, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar. Chandrasekhar was studying what happens when a star can no longer withstand its own gravity and collapses. He continued his research in the United States and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983.

Imagine someone who has spent an entire lifetime building a Lego castle. Through that castle, the person has earned the respect of many people, along with honour and social standing. One day, a young person picks up a piece that has been left on the floor and says that part of the existing castle must be dismantled. The young person has worked out where that piece belongs. How would someone who had spent a lifetime building the castle feel? Could they bring themselves to dismantle the castle they had devoted their life to building?

Hearing about quantum fluctuations in spacetime made me think that the human mind has a similar kind of instability. People's minds change with age and circumstances, and also according to their situations and interests. Sometimes people grow older and repeat the very behaviour of those who tormented them when they were young. A considerable amount of the time humanity has spent discovering and accumulating knowledge must have been wasted because of these human tendencies.

When I came across the news about the Riemann hypothesis a few days ago, I also read about another mathematician. A mathematician who had spent many years studying the Riemann hypothesis published a paper shortly after the AI-assisted research was announced. Before long, people using artificial intelligence to examine the paper pointed out several problems with it. The account also mentioned that the mathematician had conducted the research over many years without using artificial intelligence. I think that, in the future, fewer researchers will proudly announce that they did not use artificial intelligence.

When students take an examination to assess their mathematical ability, they must all solve the problems according to the same rules. The purpose is to assess each student's individual ability. When mathematicians, physicists, doctors, and engineers conduct research, I think it is better for them to have as many resources and tools available as possible. The same would apply to researchers in the social sciences and humanities. Architects could use artificial intelligence when designing buildings, and athletes could use it to analyse their movements and improve their training methods.

I think artificial intelligence could substantially reduce the time humans have to spend in fields that require a great deal of trial and error. If someone could solve in one year a problem that would otherwise have required ten years of research, that person could spend the remaining nine years investigating other problems. Human lifespans are limited. Newton and Einstein also had only so much time in their lives to conduct research. Later generations spent a great deal of time understanding and applying what they had discovered. If artificial intelligence can gradually reduce that time, humanity may be able to discover and assemble far more pieces than it does today.

I sometimes wonder what percentage of all the pieces we have discovered so far. We may have opened what we thought was a box containing a thousand Lego pieces, only to find that the object is actually made up of ten thousand or even a million pieces. Perhaps one day we will discover that the castle we have spent thousands of years assembling is only a small part of a much larger structure.
