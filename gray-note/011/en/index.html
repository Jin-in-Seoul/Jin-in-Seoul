The Book Thrown into a Black Hole

— From the Making of A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction

November 24, 2026

While writing A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction, I remembered something I had come across long ago: physicists explaining the black hole information paradox. When discussing whether information is preserved in a black hole, physicists often use a book as an example. Suppose a book is thrown into a black hole. After a very long time, when the black hole has completely evaporated, what happens to the information the book contained? If that information remains intact in the radiation emitted by the black hole, it is said that, in theory, the book could even be reconstructed. While dealing with the reconstruction of people who had died long ago in my novel, I began to think there was a problem with this analogy. Why throw a book into a black hole in the first place? A baseball or a billiard ball would have made things much easier to understand.

A book is a physical object made of paper and ink. Arrange ink in certain patterns on paper, and letters are formed. We read those letters and understand their meaning. One might recall the relationship between the signifiant and the signifié described by Saussure. This is where the word information, as used by physicists, begins to differ considerably from the same word in everyday life. The moment people hear the word book, they naturally think of the knowledge it contains or the meaning of its sentences. The information physicists speak of concerns the physical state of the matter that makes up a book, not the meaning of the sentences printed in it. I do not think the analogy of the book is wrong in itself. Physicists probably chose a book to illustrate a physical object made of paper and ink. But when using this analogy to explain their work to the general public, I think it would help to distinguish between the meaning of the sentences and what physicists call information.

Imagine throwing Kant's Critique of Pure Reason into a black hole. Would the philosophical problems Kant wrestled with in the eighteenth century still exist somewhere in the universe after the black hole had evaporated? Some people encountering this analogy for the first time might actually understand it that way. The Critique of Pure Reason contains far too many sentences, so let us print the following three on a single sheet of paper instead.

All human beings are mortal.
Socrates is a human being.
Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

If we throw this sheet of paper into a black hole, the particles making up the paper and ink will enter the black hole. Physicists are investigating what happens to the states and relationships of these particles after the black hole evaporates. Replacing the three sentences about Socrates with randomly arranged letters would make little difference to their research.

I imagined the process of reconstructing a single sheet of paper. First, it seemed necessary to decide which moment in time we wanted to reconstruct. A sheet of paper lying on a desk at 11:10 a.m. and the same sheet lying in the same place at 11:20 a.m. are in physically different states. During those ten minutes, the paper would have been in contact with the surrounding air, exchanging moisture and heat with it. Countless changes would have taken place, imperceptible to the human eye. Suppose we throw the paper towards a black hole at 11:30 a.m. These changes would continue as the paper approached the black hole. When one edge of the paper crossed the event horizon, the other edge might still be outside. A little later, the entire sheet would cross the event horizon. After the black hole had evaporated, which of these moments would the paper reconstructed by physicists belong to? They might reconstruct the paper as it was immediately before being thrown into the black hole. They might reconstruct its state just before it crossed the event horizon. Physicists can choose a particular moment for the purposes of their research. I wish they would mention that moment when telling the general public about reconstructing a book.

If information were perfectly preserved and every physical change could be calculated backwards, it might be possible to determine the state of the paper at 11:19 a.m. from its state at 11:20 a.m. By continuing backwards in the same way, we might eventually reach the moment the paper was made. We could even consider the state of the material before it was processed in the factory. We would also have to account for the air that exchanged heat with the paper and the hands of people who touched it. The space we would need to investigate in order to discover the past of a single sheet of paper would grow ever larger.

I thought of the film The Fly. The scientist played by Jeff Goldblum invents a device that disassembles an object and reconstructs it elsewhere. If we could identify the state of all the matter making up a human being and reproduce it in another location, it might be possible to send someone from Seoul to Paris. In the film, the scientist experiments on himself. The experiment appears to succeed, but because a fly entered the device with him, his body begins to undergo horrifying changes. At first, he must have regarded his reconstructed self as the result of a successful experiment. But what if his body had never begun to change? Would the scientist who was disassembled in one place and the scientist reconstructed in another be the same person? If nothing had gone wrong with him, I think I too would have accepted the two as the same person.

Let us return to the sheet of paper bearing the three sentences about Socrates. After the black hole has evaporated, a scientist succeeds in reconstructing it. The paper has the same dimensions and thickness, and the letters occupy the same positions. I take the reconstructed sheet and read the three sentences. Socrates is still mortal. While examining the paper, the scientist discovers that one atomic nucleus was omitted during reconstruction. He asks me to return the sheet. I have noticed nothing wrong with it. Not a single letter is missing, and the paper is not torn. The meaning I intended to convey through it has remained completely intact. I imagine the scientist finding the missing atomic nucleus and putting it back into the paper. When his work is finished, he hands the sheet back to me. Now he can say that he has reconstructed the paper perfectly.

Which is the paper I threw into the black hole: the one missing an atomic nucleus, or the one the scientist has finished working on? I think I would gladly accept the paper with one nucleus missing. Even if ten nuclei were missing, I would not mind. If I placed the paper on my desk and went about my life for a few days, its physical state would change again anyway.

The ancient Greeks are said to have preserved the ship that Theseus once sailed for many years. Whenever a wooden plank rotted, they replaced it with a new one. After enough time had passed for every original plank to be replaced, could people still call it the ship of Theseus? If someone had collected all the discarded planks and used them to build another ship, people might have argued over the two ships for a very long time. I considered a similar problem while writing A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction. In the novel, human beings collect records of people who died long ago and reconstruct their personalities. Among them are Immanuel Kant and Jonah Set, the founder of the Restoration Church. I included their stories in Part III. In Jonah Set's story, the problem arises from records left by the same person at different stages of his life. If something he said in his youth contradicts something he said shortly before his death, people must decide which record should take precedence.

I imagined people in the future reconstructing me after my death. If they had gathered enough records of me at the age of twenty, they might reconstruct my twenty-year-old self. They might also reconstruct me at thirty. If they reconstructed my state just before death, I would reappear in the world old and ill. My reconstructed twenty-year-old self would not even know that I had written a novel. I wonder what he would say if I gave him A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction to read. I would like to give the same book to my thirty-year-old self. I imagined the three of us sitting in a room, talking about our lives. Someone would also have to decide which of the three should inherit my name. If I had left a will before my death, which of us should be shown that will?

I had similar thoughts when I introduced a personality model of Kant into the novel. Kant developed his philosophy over many years. If we reconstructed the young Kant and the elderly Kant and seated them in the same room, an argument might break out between them as well. Suppose a scientist has succeeded in reconstructing the Critique of Pure Reason that was thrown into a black hole. What would happen if I asked that scientist to reconstruct Kant too? If Kant were reconstructed as he was before writing the Critique of Pure Reason, I would like to hand him the book. I would be very curious to see how he would read a book of his own that would not be published until later in his life.

A thought about a single book, which occurred to me while writing A Dictionary of Galactic Extinction, has led me this far. People who study difficult subjects borrow familiar words and objects from everyday life to explain their research to the general public. When choosing those objects, I think they should also consider the meanings people ordinarily attach to them.

I wish physicists had thrown a baseball into the black hole from the very beginning. Like Cy Young.
